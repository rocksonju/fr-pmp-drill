## THEME: Escalation

### ESC1
You are a newly assigned PM. A team member misses a deadline that threatens an upcoming milestone.
What do you do FIRST?
- Escalate the delay to the sponsor immediately
- *Talk to the team member to understand the root cause
- Reassign the task to a faster team member
- Log it in the issue log and move on
> Talk first. PMI expects you to address issues at the lowest level before escalating. Escalation is a last resort, not a first reflex.

### ESC2
You have tried everything within your authority to resolve a cross-department resource conflict, without success.
What is the BEST next step?
- Keep trying alone indefinitely
- Drop the resource requirement
- *Escalate to the sponsor with recommended options
- Seize the resource by force of will
> Escalation is correct once you have genuinely exhausted your own authority — and you escalate with options, not just a problem.

### ESC3
A small issue arises that a capable team member can clearly handle on their own.
Which is something the PM should NOT do?
- *Immediately escalate it to the sponsor
- Let the team member resolve it
- Offer coaching if they ask
- Monitor that it gets closed
> Don't escalate what can be solved at the lowest level. Over-escalation wastes authority and undermines the team.

### ESC4
A senior stakeholder bypasses you and hands a new requirement directly to a developer.
Select TWO appropriate first actions.
- *Meet the stakeholder to understand the underlying need
- *Reinforce the agreed communication channel through the PM
- Tell the developer to ignore the stakeholder
- Escalate the stakeholder's behavior to the sponsor
> Engage the need and restore the proper channel. You don't block the dev or jump straight to escalation.

### ESC5
Late in the project a stakeholder erupts with concerns no one saw coming, forcing an escalation.
What could have PREVENTED this?
- Escalating issues to the sponsor faster
- *Establishing clear engagement and escalation paths with stakeholders early
- Adding more status meetings after the eruption
- Ignoring the stakeholder to avoid conflict
> Proactive engagement and agreed escalation paths prevent late surprises. Prevention beats a faster reaction.

## THEME: Conflict

### CON1
Two senior analysts clash over the data schema design and the team is stalled.
What do you do FIRST?
- Pick the more experienced analyst's design
- *Bring them together to collaborate on a solution that best meets project objectives
- Escalate to their functional manager
- Impose a compromise that merges both designs
> Collaborate / problem-solve is the preferred conflict mode. Resolve toward project objectives, not favoritism or a messy compromise.

### CON2
A conflict between two team members is heating up and you do not yet know what caused it.
What do you do FIRST?
- Force a decision to end it quickly
- Smooth it over and tell them to get along
- *Gather information to understand the root cause of the conflict
- Avoid it and hope it resolves itself
> Understand before acting. You cannot resolve a conflict toward project value without first diagnosing its root cause.

### CON3
Two team members are in open conflict and the deadline is close.
Which is something the PM should NOT do?
- *Force your own solution just to end it quickly
- Work with both to understand the disagreement
- Steer the resolution toward project objectives
- Reinforce the team's ground rules
> Forcing is a lose-lose reflex. PMI favors confronting/problem-solving toward the best objective-aligned outcome, even under time pressure.

### CON4
Passive-aggressive tension is quietly lowering team morale.
Select TWO sound actions.
- *Address it openly to understand the causes
- *Reinforce the team's agreed ground rules
- Publicly punish whoever seems responsible
- Ignore it until someone formally complains
> Surface simmering conflict and lean on agreed ground rules. Don't punish blindly or wait for it to escalate.

### CON5
The same conflict keeps recurring because responsibilities are unclear.
What could PREVENT this conflict from recurring?
- Escalating to the sponsor each time it flares
- Rotating people off the team
- *Clarifying roles and responsibilities (e.g., a RACI) and revisiting ground rules
- Keeping the two people apart permanently
> Many conflicts are structural. Clarifying who owns what removes the root cause so it stops recurring.

## THEME: Change Control

### CHG1
The project plan is baselined and the client requests a "small" scope addition.
What do you do FIRST?
- Implement it immediately as good customer service
- Refuse — the baseline is locked
- *Submit a change request through integrated change control
- Add it quietly and adjust later
> Once baselined, any change to scope/schedule/cost goes through a formal change request and the CCB. Not implement-on-the-spot, not refuse.

### CHG2
The project management plan is NOT yet approved and you spot an improvement to the schedule approach.
What do you do FIRST?
- File a change request with the CCB
- Wait until the plan is approved, then change it
- *Adjust the plan directly — it is not baselined yet
- Escalate the idea to the sponsor
> Before baseline, you refine the plan directly. Change control applies only after formal approval. Don't manufacture bureaucracy that isn't required yet.

### CHG3
The scope baseline has been approved and the client asks for a change.
Which is something the PM should NOT do?
- *Implement the change immediately without a change request
- Assess the impact of the change
- Submit a formal change request
- Take it to the change control board
> After baseline, acting on a change without a CR breaks integrated change control. Assess and route it formally.

### CHG4
A change request has just been approved by the CCB.
Select TWO things to do next.
- *Update the affected baselines and plans
- *Communicate the change to stakeholders
- File a second change request to confirm
- Implement it but leave the baseline unchanged
> Approval isn't the end. Update baselines/documents and tell stakeholders so everyone works from the new truth.

### CHG5
Small, undocumented "improvements" keep slipping into the deliverables.
How do you PREVENT this unmanaged scope creep?
- Allow the small ones as goodwill
- Deliver extra features to delight the client
- *Route every change through integrated change control to protect the baseline
- Escalate to assign blame after the fact
> Unmanaged change is scope creep. Bringing all changes through change control protects the baseline and prevents compounding risk.

## THEME: Stakeholder Engagement

### STK1
Midway through execution you discover a stakeholder who was never identified.
What do you do FIRST?
- Ignore them — it is too late to add stakeholders
- Escalate the oversight to the sponsor
- *Add them to the stakeholder register, analyze them, and plan engagement
- Immediately give them whatever they ask for
> Stakeholder identification is continuous. Register, analyze power/interest, then tailor engagement. Late discovery is still managed, not ignored.

### STK2
A key stakeholder is detached and resistant during status reviews.
What do you do FIRST?
- Exclude them from future meetings
- Escalate their attitude to the sponsor
- *Tailor communication and increase engagement (e.g., face-to-face, interactive sessions)
- Proceed without their input
> Move stakeholders toward the desired engagement level with tailored, higher-touch communication. You engage resistance, you don't route around it.

### STK3
A stakeholder is resistant and disengaged.
Which is something the PM should NOT do?
- *Exclude them from future communications
- Seek to understand their concerns
- Tailor communication to their needs
- Increase engagement touchpoints
> Routing around a resistant stakeholder makes it worse. Engage and understand them rather than excluding them.

### STK4
A stakeholder complains they feel uninformed about progress.
Select TWO appropriate actions.
- *Review and adjust the communications management plan for their needs
- *Engage them to learn what information they actually expect
- Tell them to read the existing reports
- Escalate the complaint to the sponsor
> Communication is two-way and tailored. Find out their needs and adapt the plan rather than deflecting or escalating.

### STK5
An overlooked stakeholder surfaced late and forced expensive rework.
What could have PREVENTED this?
- Escalating faster once they appeared
- Sending more status emails to everyone
- *Performing thorough stakeholder identification and analysis early and continuously
- Giving them whatever they demanded on arrival
> Complete, ongoing stakeholder identification prevents late, costly surprises. Prevention over reaction.

## THEME: Agile vs Predictive

### AGL1
On an agile team, a stakeholder requests a requirement change in the middle of a sprint.
What do you do FIRST?
- Raise a formal change request
- Refuse — the sprint is locked
- *Add it to the product backlog for the Product Owner to prioritize
- Swap it into the current sprint immediately
> Agile absorbs change through the backlog and prioritization, not predictive change control. The current sprint stays protected; the PO decides ordering.

### AGL2
An agile project's sponsor asks for a traditional percent-complete status report.
What do you do FIRST?
- Build a detailed Gantt chart to satisfy them
- Refuse — agile doesn't do status
- *Show progress through working increments and information radiators (e.g., burndown, demo)
- Escalate the request as inappropriate
> Communicate progress with agile transparency tools — demos, burndown/burnup, task boards — rather than forcing predictive artifacts. Meet the need, stay agile.

### AGL3
An impediment is raised during the daily standup.
Which is something the PM should NOT do?
- *Spend the standup solving it in detail
- Note it for follow-up
- Resolve it with the right people after the standup
- Work to remove the impediment for the team
> The standup synchronizes the team; detailed problem-solving happens right after with the relevant people.

### AGL4
The team consistently fails to finish what it commits to each sprint.
Select TWO sound responses.
- *Raise it in the retrospective to inspect and adapt the root cause
- *Help the team adjust its forecast based on real velocity
- Extend the sprint length to fit the work
- Mandate overtime to hit the commitment
> Agile improves through inspect-and-adapt and realistic forecasting — not by stretching sprints or burning the team out.

### AGL5
Heavy, continuous requirements churn derailed a predictive project.
What could have PREVENTED the disruption?
- Freezing all changes after kickoff
- Adding more upfront sign-offs
- *Tailoring to an iterative/adaptive approach given the high uncertainty
- Producing a larger upfront plan
> When uncertainty is high, an adaptive approach absorbs change by design. Fit the methodology to the project.

## THEME: Servant Leadership

### SVL1
Your team is blocked by an external dependency and progress has stalled.
What do you do FIRST?
- Tell the team to figure it out themselves
- Escalate to the sponsor before anything else
- *Act to remove the impediment for the team
- Log it and wait for the dependency to clear
> A servant leader's core job is removing impediments so the team can deliver. Act on the blocker; escalate only if you genuinely cannot clear it yourself.

### SVL2
On a self-organizing team, a member asks you to assign out the day's tasks.
What do you do FIRST?
- Assign the tasks yourself to be efficient
- Escalate the team's confusion to the scrum master
- *Facilitate the team to self-organize and pull their own work
- Refuse to get involved at all
> Empower the team. Self-organizing teams pull work; the servant leader facilitates rather than commands. Empowerment is not abandonment.

### SVL3
The team has just delivered a major milestone successfully.
Which is something a servant leader should NOT do?
- *Take the credit upward for the success
- Recognize the team's contribution
- Celebrate the win with the team
- Share the credit broadly
> Servant leaders give credit to the team. Claiming it destroys trust and motivation.

### SVL4
The team is overworked and morale is dropping.
Select TWO appropriate actions.
- *Protect the team and restore a sustainable pace
- *Remove or reprioritize the overload
- Push for more hours to catch up
- Ignore it and watch the output
> Protecting the team and sustainable pace is a servant-leader duty. Burnout destroys value; don't add pressure or look away.

### SVL5
A team skills gap has repeatedly slowed delivery.
How do you PREVENT this from recurring?
- Replace the team members each time
- Do their work for them
- *Invest in training and mentoring to build the team's capability
- Escalate the slow delivery to the sponsor
> Servant leaders grow their people. Building capability removes the recurring cause rather than masking it.

## THEME: Risk and Proactivity

### RSK1
An unforeseen issue (no prior risk identified) suddenly disrupts production.
What do you do FIRST?
- Have developers change live settings immediately
- Ignore it until the next planning cycle
- *Rapidly assess the situation, then apply a workaround
- Escalate to the sponsor before doing anything
> A workaround is the response to an unidentified risk — but even a workaround follows a quick assess-then-act strategy. You never fire blindly from the hip.

### RSK2
A previously identified risk with a planned response is now occurring.
What do you do FIRST?
- Invent a new fix on the spot
- Ignore it and hope it passes
- *Implement the pre-planned risk response and engage the risk owner
- Escalate before acting
> You planned for this. Execute the agreed response and involve the assigned risk owner.

### RSK3
A new risk is identified during execution.
Which is something the PM should NOT do?
- *Ignore it until it materializes
- Add it to the risk register
- Analyze its probability and impact
- Plan an appropriate response
> Ignoring a known risk is the opposite of proactive. Register, analyze, and plan a response.

### RSK4
The same defect keeps recurring across several deliverables.
Select TWO actions that prevent recurrence.
- *Perform root-cause analysis on the defect
- *Take preventive action and update the risk register
- Keep fixing each instance as it appears
- Ignore them because they are minor
> Prevention over cure. Root-cause analysis plus preventive action stops the pattern; endless reactive fixes do not.

### RSK5
A known risk hit hard and there were no funds ready to respond.
What could have PREVENTED the scramble?
- Drawing on the management reserve reactively
- Requesting fresh budget each time a risk occurs
- *Setting aside a contingency reserve for identified risks during planning
- Skipping risk planning to save time
> Contingency reserves are planned for identified risks. Setting them aside up front prevents the funding scramble.
